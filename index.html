ChessFly

A fruit fly's mushroom body, wired exactly as in the FlyWire connectome, learns chess from nothing by playing itself.

    Speed

    Flies with white eyes play White (the classic w1118 lab mutant); red-eyed wild types play Black. If your fly is in the match, it learns from every game.

    Your fly plays against itself as fast as your computer allows. After every game, dopamine rewires the synapses between its Kenyon cells and the output neuron. Once it is an adult it practises thinking two moves ahead, which is slower but teaches it more. Now and then it sits an exam against four fixed opponents, and the result goes on the chart below.

    Inside its head

    Each lit square on the left is one input: a piece on a square, from the fly's side of the board. In a real-wired fly two or three inputs share each of its 285 projection neurons. Any Kenyon cell that gets two active projection neurons fires. The output neuron adds up the synapses of the cells that fired, and that sum is the fly's whole opinion of the position.

    What it has figured out

    Nobody told it what pieces are worth. These are the values it arrived at on its own, in pawns; textbook values in grey.

    Strength on the ChessFly scale (a random mover is 400). Dashed lines are the fixed exam opponents: Greedy grabs the biggest capture, Counter and Sentinel count material 1 and 2 moves ahead.

    ChessFly is a toy model. The projection neuron to Kenyon cell wiring is the real one from the FlyWire connectome (v783) and the learning rule comes from the insect mushroom body, but the inputs are chess pieces, not smells, and a small search is bolted on.
